---

This is one of the most clinically critical sections of your dataset. It highlights a massive gap between what people *believe* is right (their attitudes) and what they *actually do* (their practices) when navigating marriage and partner selection.

### 🧠 The Attitudes: Strict and Preventative

In theory, the respondents hold very strict, medically sound views on marriage.

* **Pre-Marital Screening:** **91.4%** believe pre-marital screening is either "Very important" or "Important."
* **Carrier-Carrier Marriage:** They are highly risk-averse. **78.3%** outright reject the idea of two carriers marrying. Only 5.0% accept it.
* **Consanguineous Marriage:** They are strongly against marrying blood relatives. **80.0%** said "Definitely not," and only 1.0% are currently in a consanguineous marriage.

*Summary:* The theoretical attitude is highly preventative. They know they should screen, and they know they should avoid high-risk marriages.

### 🏃 The Practices: The Reality Gap

However, when looking at the actual behaviors (specifically isolating the 67 married respondents in the dataset), the preventative attitudes completely break down in reality.

Among the married respondents:

* **Only 12 individuals (17.9%)** actually screened their partner *before* marriage.
* **32 individuals (47.7%)** screened their partner *after* marriage.
* **22 individuals (32.8%)** simply *did not screen* their partner at all.
* **1 individual (1.5%)** waited until pregnancy to screen.

This means that for over 80% of the married carriers in this study, the marital knot was tied *before* the genetic risk of their partner was established.

### 🤫 The Barrier: Non-Disclosure

When investigating *why* carriers failed to disclose their status to their partners before marriage, the data reveals it is rarely out of fear or malice, but rather a lack of perceived urgency:

* **"Did not think it was necessary"** (9 mentions)
* **"Lack of understanding about its importance"** (7 mentions)
* Only 3 individuals cited **"Fear of rejection"**.

**Clinical Takeaway for your Medical Studies:**
This data proves that simply telling a carrier "you must screen your partner" is a public health failure. The social momentum of getting married often overrides clinical advice. To prevent Thalassemia Major births, the Sri Lankan healthcare system cannot rely on the carriers to voluntarily initiate screening before marriage. Interventions must be systematic—such as mandating thalassemia screening as a legal prerequisite for issuing a marriage registration certificate.
