A **T-test** is specifically designed to compare the *means* (averages) of a numerical variable between exactly *two groups*.

If you want to perform a T-test (specifically an Independent Samples T-test) on your Thalassemia dataset, you need two things:

1. **A Continuous/Numerical Variable:** The thing you are measuring or scoring (e.g., Age, Test Score, Blood Pressure).
2. **A Binary Categorical Variable:** The two groups you are comparing (e.g., Male vs. Female, Treated vs. Untreated).

Based on the structure of your dataset, here are the exact aspects where a T-test would be statistically valid and useful for your research:

### 1. Comparing Age Between Two Groups

Age (`1. Age (What was your age at last birthday?):`) is the only truly continuous, natural number in your raw dataset. You can use a T-test to see if the average age differs significantly between:

* **Gender:** *Is the average age of male carriers significantly different from the average age of female carriers in this cohort?* (Grouping variable: `2. Gender:`)
* **Counseling Sufficiency:** *Are older individuals more likely to say they received sufficient counseling compared to younger individuals?* (Grouping variable: `25. After diagnosis, was counseling/information sufficient?` [Yes / No])
* **Family Disclosure:** *Is there a significant age difference between those who told their families their carrier status and those who kept it hidden?* (Grouping variable: `36. Do your family members know your thalassemia status?` [Yes / No])

### 2. Comparing Engineered "Scores" Between Two Groups

While Age is the only natural numerical column, you have a massive amount of "Tick all that apply" data. You can mathematically sum these up to create continuous "Scores," and then run T-tests on those scores.

* **Clinical Knowledge Score:** Count how many clinical forms of thalassemia (Question 16) or complications of thalassemia major (Question 27) a respondent correctly checked.
* *T-test:* Does the average Knowledge Score differ significantly between **Males and Females**?
* *T-test:* Does the average Knowledge Score differ significantly between those who are **Single vs. Married**?


* **Action/Screening Attitude Score:** Count how many proactive screening steps a respondent selected in Question 28 (e.g., getting partner tested, getting family tested).
* *T-test:* Does the average Proactive Score differ between **Sinhalese and Tamil** respondents? (Grouping variable: `3. Ethnicity:`)



### Why you CANNOT use a T-test for most of your survey

It is important to remember that a T-test compares *averages*. You cannot find the average of categories.

* You cannot run a T-test on Education Level (Up to O/L, Graduate, etc.) because you cannot calculate an "average education."
* You cannot run a T-test comparing three or more groups (like checking Age against the three Diagnosis Locations: School, Work, Community). If you have three or more groups, you must upgrade to an **ANOVA (Analysis of Variance)** test.
