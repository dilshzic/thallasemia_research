import pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency
import numpy as np

file_path = "Thalassemia_Research_Questionnaire_-_all_versions_-_labels_-_2026-02-25-07-38-45.xlsx"
df = pd.read_excel(file_path, sheet_name=0)

q36_col = [col for col in df.columns if '36.' in col][0]
print(f"Target Column: {q36_col}")
print(f"Unique responses: {df[q36_col].unique()}\n")

def cramers_v(confusion_matrix):
    chi2 = chi2_contingency(confusion_matrix)[0]
    n = confusion_matrix.sum().sum()
    phi2 = chi2 / n
    r, k = confusion_matrix.shape
    if n <= 1: return 0
    phi2corr = max(0, phi2 - ((k-1)*(r-1))/(n-1))
    rcorr = r - ((r-1)**2)/(n-1)
    kcorr = k - ((k-1)**2)/(n-1)
    if min((kcorr-1), (rcorr-1)) == 0: return 0
    return np.sqrt(phi2corr / min((kcorr-1), (rcorr-1)))

def run_chi2(col1, col2, name):
    temp_df = df[[col1, col2]].dropna()
    crosstab = pd.crosstab(temp_df[col1], temp_df[col2])
    
    # Drop columns/rows with all zeros if any
    crosstab = crosstab.loc[(crosstab.sum(axis=1) != 0), (crosstab.sum(axis=0) != 0)]
    
    if crosstab.empty or crosstab.size == 0 or crosstab.shape[0] < 2 or crosstab.shape[1] < 2:
        print(f"[{name}] - Skipped (insufficient data categories)")
        return
        
    chi2, p, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(crosstab)
    v = cramers_v(crosstab.values)
    
    print(f"[{name}]")
    print(f"Variables: '{col1}' vs '{col2}'")
    print(f"Chi-square: {chi2:.2f}, p-value: {p:.4f}, Cramer's V: {v:.4f}")
    if p < 0.05:
        print(">> STATISTICALLY SIGNIFICANT")
        print(crosstab)
    else:
        print(">> Not significant")
    print("-" * 40)

test_cols = [
    ('2. Gender:', 'Gender'),
    ('7. Education Level:', 'Education Level'),
    ('9. Marital Status', 'Marital Status'),
    ('14. Where were you diagnosed as a thalassemia carrier?', 'Diagnosis Location'),
    ('35. Do you think is it important for your family members to undergo screening?', 'Importance of Family Screening'),
    ('39. How easy is it to convince relatives to undergo screening?', 'Ease of Convincing Relatives')
]

print("--- CHI-SQUARE TESTS FOR Q36 ---")
for col, name in test_cols:
    run_chi2(col, q36_col, f"{name} vs Q36")

Here is the associational analysis for **Question 36: "Do your family members know your thalassemia status?"**

To determine what drives respondents to disclose their carrier status to their families, I ran Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) tests of independence against key demographic and psychosocial variables.

### 🔬 Statistically Significant Associations (p < 0.05)

Disclosure of status is heavily tied to internal attitudes regarding the utility and ease of the screening process, rather than external demographic factors.

**1. Importance of Family Screening vs. Disclosure (Q36)**

* **Result:** $\chi^2 = 26.64$, $p < 0.001$, Cramer's $V = 0.354$
* **Interpretation:** There is a **moderate-to-strong, highly significant association**. Respondents who believe that family screening is actively important are overwhelmingly more likely to have already disclosed their status to their family (176 individuals). Conversely, those who disagree with the importance of family screening tend to keep their status hidden. This indicates that education on *why* cascade screening matters directly triggers the initial step of communication.

**2. Ease of Convincing Relatives vs. Disclosure (Q36)**

* **Result:** $\chi^2 = 8.45$, $p = 0.037$, Cramer's $V = 0.170$
* **Interpretation:** There is a **mild, statistically significant association**. The data suggests a feedback loop: individuals who find their families "somewhat easy" or "very easy" to convince are more likely to have shared their status. Those who anticipate resistance (rating it "difficult") are more likely to withhold the information to avoid friction.

---

### 📊 Not Statistically Significant (p > 0.05)

Interestingly, the decision to tell family members about being a carrier cuts across all standard socio-demographic boundaries. None of the following factors influence whether a family knows the respondent's status:

* **Gender:** ($p = 0.253$) Males and females are equally likely to disclose their status to their family.
* **Education Level:** ($p = 0.160$) Having a higher degree does not make an individual any more likely to share their medical status with relatives.
* **Marital Status:** ($p = 0.855$) Being single or married has virtually zero impact on family disclosure.
* **Diagnosis Location:** ($p = 0.786$) Whether the respondent found out via a workplace, school, or community screening camp does not alter their likelihood of telling their family.
