## **Results**

### Demographic Profile

A cross-sectional analysis was conducted on a cohort of 201 identified thalassemia carriers. The mean age of the participants was 29.9 years. The cohort exhibited a female predominance (57.7%), and the majority of respondents were unmarried (65.2%). Educational attainment within the cohort was notably high, with 81.1% having completed education up to the GCE Advanced Level or higher. Geographically, the highest concentration of respondents resided in the Western (59.7%) and North Western (20.4%) provinces of Sri Lanka.

### Baseline Clinical Knowledge: Surface vs. Deep Understanding

The cohort demonstrated a high degree of "surface-level" disease awareness. A significant majority correctly identified thalassemia as a hereditary, blood-related condition (90.0%) and acknowledged that its transmission is preventable (75.1%).

However, an analysis of "deep" clinical knowledge revealed substantial gaps regarding the systemic pathophysiology and prognosis of Thalassemia Major. While 84.1% recognized the necessity of lifelong blood transfusions, awareness of secondary systemic complications was markedly low: fatigue (31.8%), iron overload affecting the heart and liver (26.4%), delayed growth and puberty (23.9%), bone deformities (20.4%), and endocrine complications such as diabetes and infertility (7.5%). Furthermore, profound misconceptions existed regarding curability, with 47.8% falsely believing the condition is entirely incurable and 33.3% reporting they did not know, while only 14.9% correctly identified bone marrow transplantation as a highly complex curative option.

### Attitudes versus Practices in Partner Screening

A critical discrepancy was observed between theoretical attitudes and actual preventative practices regarding marriage. While 91.4% of the total cohort affirmed that pre-marital thalassemia screening is either "Important" or "Very important," real-world execution among the married subgroup ($n=67$) contradicted this sentiment.

Among married respondents, only 17.9% underwent partner screening *before* marriage. The majority screened their partners *after* marriage (47.7%) or did not screen them at all (32.8%). A Chi-Square test of independence confirmed a highly significant association between marital status and screening practices ($\chi^2 = 86.81$, $p < 0.001$, Cramer's $V = 0.812$). The primary barriers to pre-marital disclosure and screening were not driven by social stigma, but rather a lack of perceived necessity and inadequate understanding of the screening's importance.

### Cascade Screening Penetration and Barriers

The efficacy of cascade screening within families demonstrated a severe inverse relationship with genetic distance. While respondents achieved high screening rates among first-degree relatives (51.3% reported *all* first-degree relatives were screened), penetration plummeted in extended families. For third-degree relatives (cousins), 83.9% of respondents were unaware of their family members' screening status.

When investigating the barriers to cascade screening, respondents primarily cited a "Lack of knowledge" among relatives (48.8%) and the "Perception that screening was unnecessary" (25.4%). Conversely, logistical constraints such as cost and access (7.0%) or fear of results (3.5%) were negligible factors. Furthermore, statistical analysis revealed significant regional disparities in family disclosure; respondents in the Western and North Western provinces were highly likely to disclose their status, whereas those in the Sabaragamuwa and Central provinces exhibited higher rates of non-disclosure ($\chi^2 = 40.54$, $p < 0.001$, Cramer's $V = 0.407$).

## **Discussion**

### The Knowledge-Action Gap in Pre-Marital Screening

The findings of this study highlight a systemic vulnerability in current thalassemia prevention strategies. The data explicitly proves that high educational attainment and theoretical disease awareness do not naturally translate into preventative marital practices. The substantial delay in partner screening—where over 80% of married carriers established marital ties before verifying their partner's genetic risk—indicates that relying on voluntary, patient-initiated pre-marital screening is insufficient. Public health frameworks must transition from purely educational campaigns to systematic, policy-driven interventions, potentially integrating mandatory thalassemia screening protocols into standard marriage registration processes.

### Addressing the Deficit in Clinical Health Literacy

While fundamental public health messaging has successfully conveyed that thalassemia is a preventable blood disorder, it has failed to communicate the clinical severity of the disease. The profound lack of awareness regarding iron overload, endocrine failure, and bone deformities suggests that carriers do not fully grasp the lifelong morbidity associated with a Thalassemia Major birth. Enhancing the depth of post-diagnostic counseling to include the systemic toll of the disease could serve as a vital psychological catalyst, increasing the urgency carriers feel to ensure their partners are screened preemptively.

### Optimizing Cascade Screening Frameworks

The sharp decline in cascade screening among second and third-degree relatives identifies a critical breakdown in community-level prevention. The primary barriers identified—lack of knowledge and the perception that asymptomatic individuals do not require testing—suggest that the burden of educating extended families should not rest solely on the diagnosed carrier. The statistical evidence indicates that carriers find it difficult to convince relatives to undergo screening. Therefore, institutional support mechanisms, such as formalized genetic counseling letters or healthcare-provider-initiated family tracing, are necessary to bypass intra-family communication barriers. Furthermore, targeted destigmatization campaigns are urgently required in regions like the Sabaragamuwa and Central provinces to facilitate open familial dialogue.
