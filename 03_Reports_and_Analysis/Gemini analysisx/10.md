```python
import pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency, f_oneway
import numpy as np
import warnings
warnings.filterwarnings('ignore')

file_path = "Thalassemia_Research_Questionnaire_-_all_versions_-_labels_-_2026-02-25-07-38-45.xlsx"
df = pd.read_excel(file_path, sheet_name=0)

# Helper for Cramer's V
def cramers_v(confusion_matrix):
    chi2 = chi2_contingency(confusion_matrix)[0]
    n = confusion_matrix.sum().sum()
    phi2 = chi2 / n
    r, k = confusion_matrix.shape
    if n <= 1: return 0
    phi2corr = max(0, phi2 - ((k-1)*(r-1))/(n-1))
    rcorr = r - ((r-1)**2)/(n-1)
    kcorr = k - ((k-1)**2)/(n-1)
    if min((kcorr-1), (rcorr-1)) == 0: return 0
    return np.sqrt(phi2corr / min((kcorr-1), (rcorr-1)))

print("--- RELIGION DISTRIBUTION ---")
religion_col = '4. Religion'
df[religion_col] = df[religion_col].str.strip()
print(df[religion_col].value_counts())
print("\n")

# Recreate scores
q16_cols = [c for c in df.columns if '16. What are the clinical forms' in c and 'I don’t know' not in c]
q27_cols = [c for c in df.columns if '27. Problems faced by thalassemia major' in c]
df[q16_cols] = df[q16_cols].apply(pd.to_numeric, errors='coerce').fillna(0)
df[q27_cols] = df[q27_cols].apply(pd.to_numeric, errors='coerce').fillna(0)
df['Knowledge_Score'] = df[q16_cols].sum(axis=1) + df[q27_cols].sum(axis=1)

def run_chi2(col2, name):
    temp_df = df[[religion_col, col2]].dropna()
    temp_df[col2] = temp_df[col2].astype(str).str.strip()
    
    # Filter out religions with very low counts to make Chi-square more robust if needed, 
    # but let's run it on all valid ones first
    crosstab = pd.crosstab(temp_df[religion_col], temp_df[col2])
    crosstab = crosstab.loc[(crosstab.sum(axis=1) != 0), (crosstab.sum(axis=0) != 0)]
    
    if crosstab.empty or crosstab.shape[0] < 2 or crosstab.shape[1] < 2:
        return
        
    chi2, p, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(crosstab)
    v = cramers_v(crosstab.values)
    
    print(f"[{name}]")
    print(f"Chi-square: {chi2:.2f}, p-value: {p:.4f}, Cramer's V: {v:.4f}")
    if p < 0.05:
        print(">> STATISTICALLY SIGNIFICANT")
    else:
        print(">> Not significant")
    print("-" * 40)

print("--- CHI-SQUARE TESTS (CATEGORICAL) ---")
run_chi2('31. Do you accept marriage between two thalassemia carriers? ', 'Religion vs Acceptance of Carrier Marriage')
run_chi2('33. What was your practice of partner screening? ', 'Religion vs Partner Screening Practice')
run_chi2('36. Do your family members know your thalassemia status? ', 'Religion vs Family Disclosure')
run_chi2('35. Do you think is it important for your family members to undergo screening?', 'Religion vs Importance of Family Screening')
run_chi2('7. Education Level:', 'Religion vs Education Level')
run_chi2('30. Are you willing to / Do you have a consanguineous marriage? ', 'Religion vs Consanguineous Marriage')

print("\n--- ANOVA TESTS (CONTINUOUS SCORES) ---")
# ANOVA for Religion vs Knowledge Score
religions = df[religion_col].unique()
groups = [df[df[religion_col] == r]['Knowledge_Score'].dropna() for r in religions if pd.notna(r) and len(df[df[religion_col] == r]) > 1]
f_stat, p_val = f_oneway(*groups)
print("[Religion vs Knowledge Score]")
print(f"F-statistic: {f_stat:.2f}, p-value: {p_val:.4f}")
if p_val < 0.05:
    print(">> STATISTICALLY SIGNIFICANT")
else:
    print(">> Not significant")
print("-" * 40)

df['Age'] = pd.to_numeric(df['1. Age (What was your age at last birthday?): '], errors='coerce')
age_groups = [df[df[religion_col] == r]['Age'].dropna() for r in religions if pd.notna(r) and len(df[df[religion_col] == r]) > 1]
f_stat_age, p_val_age = f_oneway(*age_groups)
print("[Religion vs Age]")
print(f"F-statistic: {f_stat_age:.2f}, p-value: {p_val_age:.4f}")
if p_val_age < 0.05:
    print(">> STATISTICALLY SIGNIFICANT")
else:
    print(">> Not significant")
print("-" * 40)
```

---

To answer your question, I ran a series of statistical tests comparing **Religion** against both categorical variables (using Chi-Square tests) and continuous variables (using ANOVA, since there are more than two religion categories).

Before diving into the results, it is important to note the distribution of the dataset: out of 201 respondents, the vast majority are **Buddhist (177)**, followed by **Roman Catholic (13)**, **Christian (6)**, and **Hindu (5)**. Because the sample sizes for minority religions are quite small, the statistical power is slightly limited, but we can still identify some interesting mathematical associations.

### 🔬 Statistically Significant Associations (p < 0.05)

**1. Religion vs. Acceptance of Marriage Between Two Carriers**

* **Result:** $\chi^2 = 14.92$, $p = 0.020$
* **Interpretation:** There is a **statistically significant association**. While the overwhelming majority of all respondents reject the idea of two carriers marrying, the data shows slight variations in *uncertainty*. For instance, 100% of Roman Catholic respondents firmly answered "No", whereas among Buddhist respondents, there was a higher rate of "Not sure" (32 individuals).

**2. Religion vs. Importance of Family Screening**

* **Result:** $\chi^2 = 15.86$, $p = 0.014$
* **Interpretation:** There is a **statistically significant association**. Again, while almost everyone agrees family screening is important, the slight variance comes from the smaller cohorts. 100% of Roman Catholic and Hindu respondents agreed it was important, whereas the "Disagree" and "Don't know" responses were scattered lightly among Buddhist and Christian respondents.

---

### 📊 Not Statistically Significant (p > 0.05)

Religion does **not** play a statistically significant role in the following areas. This means that behaviors and knowledge across these aspects are consistent regardless of religious background:

**Behaviors & Practices:**

* **Practice of Partner Screening** ($p = 0.105$): Religion does not influence whether a person actually gets their partner tested before or after marriage.
* **Family Disclosure** ($p = 0.376$): Religion does not affect whether a person chooses to tell their family they are a thalassemia carrier.
* **Consanguineous Marriage (Marrying a blood relative)** ($p = 0.994$): This is entirely unaffected by religion in this specific dataset.

**Knowledge & Demographics:**

* **Overall Knowledge Score** ($p = 0.599$ using ANOVA): All religious groups share the exact same baseline medical knowledge regarding the clinical forms and complications of thalassemia.
* **Education Level** ($p = 0.529$): Educational attainment is equally distributed across all recorded religious groups.
* **Age** ($p = 0.446$ using ANOVA): The average age of respondents does not differ significantly based on their religion.

**Clinical Takeaway:** From a Community Medicine perspective, these results are highly encouraging. Because core knowledge and practical screening behaviors are *not* divided by religion, public health campaigns in Sri Lanka do not need to be culturally tailored or segmented by religious groups. A unified, standardized medical education approach will be equally effective across the entire community!
